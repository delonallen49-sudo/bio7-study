26.1

Phylogenies show evolutionary relationships

Lecture video 26.1 (opens online)
Big idea
Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a species or group of related species, and systematics is the discipline that classifies organisms and determines their evolutionary relationships. Linnaeus (18th century) gave us binomial nomenclature and hierarchical classification, both still used. A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis about evolutionary relationships: branch points are common ancestors, and the branching pattern (not the order of the tips) shows the order in which lineages diverged. Trees show patterns of descent, not phenotypic similarity, and do not show when species evolved or how much change occurred.

Must know

  • Phylogeny: evolutionary history of a species or group of related species.
  • Systematics: discipline focused on classifying organisms and determining their evolutionary relationships.
  • Carl Linnaeus, 18th century, based his taxonomy on morphology (resemblance). Two features still used: two-part species names (binomial nomenclature) and hierarchical classification.
  • Binomial = genus + specific epithet. Genus is capitalized, specific epithet is not; the whole name is italicized when typed and underlined when handwritten.
  • Example binomial: Panthera pardus (leopard/panther). Panthera is the genus, pardus is the specific epithet.
  • Hierarchy from broad to narrow: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Memorize the order.
  • A group at any level is a taxon (plural taxa). Broader taxa are not comparable between lineages (an order of snails does not have the same diversity as an order of mammals).
  • Linnaean placements do not always reflect evolutionary relationships because the system was built on morphology. Systematists want only groups that include a common ancestor and all its descendants to be named.
  • Branch point (node): divergence of two lineages from a common ancestor.
  • Evolutionary lineage: sequence of ancestral organisms leading to a particular descendant taxon.
  • Sister taxa: groups that share an immediate common ancestor not shared by any other group (chimps and humans in the lecture figure).
  • Branches can be rotated around a branch point without changing relationships. Trees can be drawn horizontally, vertically, or diagonally.
  • Rooted tree: includes a branch representing the most recent common ancestor of all taxa in the tree.
  • Basal taxon: a lineage that diverges from all other members of the group early in the group's history (fishes in the lecture figure).
  • You cannot assume a taxon evolved from the taxon next to it on the tree.

A named group at any level of the hierarchy.

A branching diagram that represents a hypothesis about the evolutionary history of a group.
branch point (node)
The point where two lineages diverge from a common ancestor.

A sequence of ancestral organisms leading to a particular descendant taxon.
sister taxa
Groups that share an immediate common ancestor that is not shared by any other group.

A tree that includes a branch for the most recent common ancestor of all taxa in the tree.
basal taxon
A lineage that diverges early in the history of the group, off on its own branch near the root.
polytomy
A branch point from which more than two lineages emerge; it means the order of divergence is not yet resolved. (Term assigned by the study guide request; not stated in the lecture audio.)

Examples

  • Lizard tree: geckos kept limbs; snakes lost limbs (hash mark); glass lizards also lost limbs independently. Glass lizards resemble snakes but are not closely related to them.
  • Panthera pardus: genus Panthera, family Felidae, order Carnivora, class Mammalia, phylum Chordata, kingdom Animalia, domain Eukarya.
  • Order Carnivora tree: Felidae (Panthera pardus), Mustelidae (Taxidea taxus American badger, Lutra lutra European otter), Canidae (Canis latrans coyote, Canis lupus gray wolf).
  • Snakes lost limbs, so a morphology-only system would not group them with other reptiles; DNA evidence supports reclassification.
  • Chimps and humans are sister taxa; switching their positions changes nothing.

26.2

Phylogenies are inferred from morphological and molecular data

Lecture video 26.2 (opens online)
Big idea
Systematists use morphology, genes (DNA and protein sequences), and biochemistry to infer phylogeny. Only features that result from common ancestry (homologies) are useful. Analogy is similarity due to convergent evolution, and it must be sorted out from homology before building a tree. The more complex the shared similarity, the more likely it reflects common ancestry.

Must know

  • Common ancestry is a MUST: only features resulting from common ancestry are useful for building trees.
  • Homology: phenotypic and genetic similarity due to shared ancestry.
  • Analogy: similarity due to convergent evolution, not common ancestry.
  • Convergent evolution: unrelated species evolve superficially similar features because natural selection acts in similar environments.
  • Organisms with similar morphology or DNA sequence are likely more closely related than those very different in structure and sequence.
  • Morphology and genetics do not always match: species can look very different but be genetically similar (Hawaiian silverswords), or vice versa.
  • More complex similarity (for example, many shared portions of nucleotide sequence) means homology is more likely.
  • Coincidental sequence matches happen; statistical tools and databases score molecular homology and separate distant homology from coincidence.

Terms

homology
Similarity (phenotypic or genetic) due to shared ancestry.
analogy
Similarity due to convergent evolution rather than shared ancestry.
convergent evolution
Independent evolution of similar features in unrelated species exposed to similar selection pressures.
homologous structures
Structures similar because they were inherited from a common ancestor, even if they now serve different functions.
analogous structures
Structures with similar function or appearance that arose by convergent evolution, not common ancestry.

Examples

  • Hawaiian silverswords: look very different (tall twiggy trees to dense ground-hugging shrubs) depending on the island, but are genetically very similar.
  • Australian mole and African golden mole: look alike and both dig tunnels, but similarity is analogy from similar lifestyles, not shared ancestry.
  • Video: forelimbs of human, bat, and whale are homologous (four limbs in a shared ancestor).
  • Video: grasshopper and butterfly mouthparts and antennae are homologous.
  • Video: North American cactus (Astrophytum) and African Euphorbia look alike (spherical, eight symmetrical sectors, store water) by convergent evolution.
  • Video: bird wings and insect wings are analogous; bird wings contain bones homologous to human arm bones, insect wings have no bones.
What she stressed in lecture
  • 'Analogy is similarity due to convergent evolution. Homology is similarity due to shared ancestry.'
  • 'The more similarity between complex structures, the more likely they evolved from a common ancestor.'
  • Video: the first step in building a tree is to distinguish homologous from analogous structures.
Memory hooks
  • HOMology = same HOMeHomology means similar because of a shared ancestor.
  • ANAlogy = ANother AncestorAnalogy means similar because of convergent evolution, not common ancestry.
  • Look alike, live alike, not kinThe Australian mole and the African golden mole look alike only because they live alike (analogy).
  • The more complex the match, the more likely it's familyComplex shared similarities, like long stretches of matching DNA, are more likely to be homology.

26.3

Shared characters are used to construct phylogenetic trees

Lecture video 26.3 (opens online)
Big idea
Cladistics groups organisms by common ancestry into clades, which are monophyletic (ancestor plus all descendants). Paraphyletic groups leave out some descendants, and polyphyletic groups leave out the most recent common ancestor. Trees are built from shared derived characters, identified by comparing an ingroup with an outgroup. Maximum parsimony picks the tree with the fewest evolutionary events, and maximum likelihood picks the tree most probable under rules of DNA change.

Must know

  • Cladistics: organisms grouped primarily by common ancestry.
  • Clade: an ancestral species and ALL of its descendants. Clades can be nested within larger clades.
  • Monophyletic group: ancestor and all its descendants; only monophyletic groups are clades.
  • Paraphyletic group: ancestral species and some, but not all, of its descendants. The common ancestor IS in the group.
  • Polyphyletic group: includes distantly related species but not their most recent common ancestor.
  • Know Figure 26.10 and be able to give and explain an example of each group type.
  • Shared ancestral character: originated in an ancestor of the taxon.
  • Shared derived character: an evolutionary novelty unique to a particular clade. Losses count too (limb loss in snakes or whales).
  • Ancestral vs derived is relative: a backbone is ancestral within vertebrates but derived when separating vertebrates from other animals.
  • Outgroup: closely related to, but not part of, the group being studied. Ingroup: the group being studied.
  • Characters shared by outgroup and ingroup are assumed ancestral. Each derived character is assumed to arise only once in the ingroup.
  • Branch lengths can be proportional to genetic change, or proportional to time (calibrated with fossil data).
  • Maximum parsimony: the most likely tree requires the fewest evolutionary events; for DNA, the fewest base changes.
  • Maximum likelihood: the tree most likely to have produced the DNA data, based on probability rules about how DNA changes (for example, assuming all substitutions equally likely).
  • Computer programs search for trees that are both parsimonious and likely. The goal is the most likely tree, not a perfect tree.

Terms

cladistics
An approach that classifies organisms by common ancestry.
clade
A group made of an ancestral species and all of its descendants.
monophyletic
A group containing an ancestor and all of its descendants.
paraphyletic
A group containing an ancestor and some, but not all, of its descendants.
polyphyletic
A group of distantly related species that does not include their most recent common ancestor.
shared ancestral character
A character that originated in an ancestor of the taxon being studied.
shared derived character
An evolutionary novelty unique to a particular clade.
outgroup
A species or group closely related to, but not part of, the group being studied.
ingroup
The group of species being studied.
maximum parsimony
The principle that the best tree needs the fewest evolutionary events (fewest base changes for DNA).
maximum likelihood
The principle that the best tree is the one most likely to produce the data, given probability rules for DNA change.

Examples

  • Paraphyletic: even-toed ungulates plus hippopotamuses, leaving out cetaceans (which share their ancestor). Adding cetaceans makes it monophyletic.
  • Polyphyletic: cetaceans plus seals grouped because they look alike; their most recent common ancestor is left out.
  • Character table (lancelet outgroup; lamprey, bass, frog, turtle, leopard ingroup): characters are vertebral column, hinged jaws, four limbs, amnion, hair. 0 = absent, 1 = present. Lancelet has none. Vertebral column: lamprey onward. Hinged jaws: bass onward. Four limbs: frog, turtle, leopard. Amnion: turtle and leopard. Hair: leopard only.
  • Branch lengths proportional to genetic change: Drosophila (fruit fly) has the longest branch; lancelet, zebrafish, frog, chicken, human, mouse follow.
  • Lost features as shared derived characters: loss of limbs in snakes or whales.

26.4

An organism's evolutionary history is documented in its genome

Lecture video 26.4 (opens online)
Big idea
Molecular comparisons reveal relatedness among groups that are morphologically dissimilar or poorly represented in the fossil record. Different genes evolve at different rates: rRNA genes change slowly (ancient relationships) and mitochondrial DNA changes fast (recent events). Gene duplication creates gene families, and homologous genes are either orthologous (from speciation) or paralogous (from duplication within a species).

Must know

  • Molecular data help with groups that are morphologically dissimilar or poorly represented in the fossil record.
  • DNA coding for ribosomal RNA (rRNA) changes very slowly: used for ancient relationships.
  • Mitochondrial DNA evolves relatively rapidly: used for more recent evolutionary events.
  • Gene duplication increases the number of genes and gives more opportunity for evolutionary change. Repeated duplications produce gene families.
  • Orthologous genes: homology from a speciation event; found in different species. Used to infer phylogeny because they reflect the history of speciation.
  • Paralogous genes: homology from gene duplication; found as copies within one species; can diverge within that species.
  • Lineages that diverged long ago still share many orthologous genes. Humans and mice diverged about 65 million years ago, and 99% of their genes are orthologous.

Terms

gene duplication
Copying of a gene that increases the number of genes in a genome.
gene family
A group of related genes within a genome produced by repeated gene duplications.
orthologous genes
Homologous genes in different species whose homology results from speciation.
paralogous genes
Homologous genes within one species whose homology results from gene duplication.

Examples

  • Cytochrome c genes (orthologous): same function in humans and dogs, but sequences have diverged since their last common ancestor.
  • Human olfactory receptor gene family (paralogous): copies diverged to make proteins that respond to many different molecules.
  • Humans and mice: diverged about 65 million years ago; 99% of genes orthologous.
What she stressed in lecture
  • Both orthologous and paralogous genes are types of homologous genes.
  • 'Orthologous genes are what is ultimately used for inferring phylogeny, because they actually reflect the history of speciation events.'
  • Choice of rRNA vs mitochondrial DNA depends on which point in evolutionary time you want to look at.
Memory hooks
  • ORTHO = Other species; PARA = PAiR in one genomeOrthologous genes come from speciation and are found in different species. Paralogous genes come from duplication and are copies within one species.
  • Ribosomal = Remote past; Mitochondrial = More recentrRNA genes change very slowly, so they show ancient splits. mtDNA changes fast, so it shows recent events.
  • Copy, then changeGene duplication makes extra copies, which are free to evolve into gene families.

26.5

Molecular clocks help track evolutionary time

Lecture video 26.5 (opens online)
Big idea
A molecular clock estimates the absolute time of evolutionary change, based on the observation that some genes evolve at a constant rate. It assumes the number of nucleotide substitutions is proportional to time since the split from a common ancestor (orthologs) or since gene duplication (paralogs). Clocks are calibrated against branch-point dates from the fossil record, and they have limits because some genes change in bursts and rates differ among genes and taxa.

Must know

  • Molecular clock: approach to estimate the absolute time of evolutionary change, based on genes that appear to evolve at a constant rate.
  • Assumption: number of nucleotide substitutions is proportional to the time since the split from a common ancestor (orthologous genes) or since gene duplication (paralogous genes).
  • Calibration: graph the number of genetic differences against dates of branch points known from the fossil record; the inferred rate estimates dates not visible in the fossil record.
  • Limits: some genes evolve in irregular bursts; rate deviates from the average periodically; the same gene may evolve at different rates in different taxa; some clocklike genes evolve at very different rates from each other.
  • Neutral mutations are neither beneficial nor harmful. If most mutations are neutral, molecular change should be regular like a clock.
  • If an amino acid sequence is critical to survival, mutations are harmful and change is slow. If it is less critical, more mutations are neutral and change is faster.
  • Irregularities come from natural selection favoring some mutations over others.
  • Estimates older than the fossil record have a high degree of uncertainty. Using multiple genes may improve estimates.

Terms

molecular clock
A method that uses constant rates of molecular change to estimate the absolute time of evolutionary events.
neutral mutation
A mutation that is neither beneficial nor detrimental.
calibration
Setting the clock rate by graphing genetic differences against fossil-dated branch points.

Examples

  • Irregular bursts of change compared to punctuated equilibrium from the first exam.
What she stressed in lecture
  • 'Concept 26.5 is a little bit difficult to fathom... make sure you read about this in the textbook.'
  • Estimates older than the fossil record 'cannot be relied upon. They have a very high degree of uncertainty.'
Memory hooks
  • Neutral mutations tick like a clockIf most mutations are neutral, they pile up at a steady rate, and that is the molecular clock.
  • Vital genes barely tickSequences critical to survival change slowly, because most mutations in them are harmful.
  • Set the clock with fossilsCalibration: plot genetic differences against fossil-dated branch points, then use that rate to date splits that have no fossils.

26.6

Our understanding of the tree of life continues to change based on new data

Lecture video 26.6 (opens online)
Big idea
Classification moved from two kingdoms (plants and animals) to five kingdoms by the 1960s (Monera, Protista, Plantae, Fungi, Animalia) to the current three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya). Prokaryotes form two domains because some differ from each other as much as from eukaryotes, and Archaea are more closely related to Eukarya than to Bacteria. Horizontal gene transfer moved genes between genomes and even between domains, so the early tree of life looks more like a tangled web than a clean branching tree.

Must know

  • Two kingdoms: plants and animals. Fungi and prokaryotes were placed with plants (fungi are sedentary; prokaryotes have cell walls).
  • Five kingdoms (by the 1960s): Monera (prokaryotes), Protista, Plantae, Fungi, Animalia.
  • Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Domain is a level above kingdom.
  • Bacteria: most known prokaryotes. Archaea: diverse prokaryotes in a wide variety of environments. Eukarya: single-celled and multicellular organisms with true nuclei.
  • Eukarya includes kingdoms Plantae, Fungi, Animalia. Protista is no longer a kingdom because some protists are more closely related to plants, fungi, or animals than to other protists.
  • Domain Archaea is more closely related to domain Eukarya than to domain Bacteria.
  • Lokiarchaeota (recently discovered archaea) may be the sister group of eukaryotes.
  • Horizontal gene transfer: movement of genes from one genome to another, between species or even between domains. Mechanisms: exchange of transposable elements, plasmids, viral infection, possibly fusion of organisms.
  • Disparities between trees built from different genes result from horizontal gene transfer.
  • Horizontal gene transfer played a key role in the evolution of both prokaryotes and eukaryotes and was probably common from the very beginning.

Terms

domain
The broadest taxonomic category, above kingdom: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
Monera
Former kingdom that held all prokaryotes in the five-kingdom system.
horizontal gene transfer
Movement of genes from one genome to another, between species or even domains.

Examples

  • The red alga Galdieria sulphuraria acquired about 5% of its genes from bacteria and archaea.
  • rRNA gene tree: eukaryotes and archaea are more closely related to each other than to bacteria.
  • Tangled 'highway' diagram of ancestral cell populations exchanging genes, instead of a clean dichotomous tree.
What she stressed in lecture
  • 'There is a concept that I do want you to know called horizontal gene transfer.'
  • 'Domain archaea is actually more closely related to domain eukarya than it is to domain bacteria.'
  • 'Our classification system is manmade', and it will keep changing as molecular data grow.
  • 'Just because two things might look the same... it doesn't mean that they're closely related.'
Memory hooks
  • 2 → 5 → 3Classification went from 2 kingdoms, to 5 kingdoms (1960s), to 3 domains.
  • My Pretty Purple Fat AntThe five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Plantae, Fungi, Animalia.
  • B-A-E: Archaea stand next to EukaryaThree domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Archaea are more closely related to Eukarya than to Bacteria.
  • Horizontal = handed sidewaysHorizontal gene transfer passes genes sideways between species (even between domains), not down from parent to offspring.

27.1

Structural and functional adaptations contribute to prokaryotic success

Lecture video 27.1 (opens online)
Big idea
Prokaryotes (domains Bacteria and Archaea) were the first organisms on Earth and are the most abundant. They are small, mostly unicellular, and come as cocci, bacilli, and spirals. Surface structures (cell wall, capsule, fimbriae, pili, endospores) and flagella help them survive; they lack membrane-bound organelles and carry one circular chromosome in a nucleoid, plus plasmids. They reproduce by binary fission with short generation times.

Must know

  • Size: about 0.5 to 5 micrometers, vs 10 to 100 micrometers for eukaryotic cells.
  • Shapes: cocci (spheres), bacilli (rods), spirals (corkscrew or S shape).
  • Cell wall maintains shape, protects the cell, and prevents bursting in a hypotonic environment.
  • Salt preserves food because water loss (plasmolysis) slows the growth of food-spoiling prokaryotes.
  • Eukaryotic walls: cellulose (plants) or chitin (fungi). Bacterial walls contain peptidoglycan: sugars cross-linked by polypeptides.
  • Archaeal walls contain polysaccharides and proteins but lack peptidoglycan.
  • Gram stain classifies bacteria by cell wall composition. Gram-positive: thick peptidoglycan layer, stains purple. Gram-negative: thin peptidoglycan between membranes plus an outer lipopolysaccharide layer, stains pink/red.
  • Gram-negative bacteria are more resistant to antibiotics because the outer membrane blocks drugs from reaching the peptidoglycan.
  • Many antibiotics target peptidoglycan. Human cells have no peptidoglycan, so these antibiotics do not harm our cells.
  • Capsule: dense, well-defined sticky layer of polysaccharide or protein outside the wall (a slime layer if less organized). Functions: adherence to substrate, prevents dehydration, protects from the host immune system.
  • Endospores: metabolically inactive, resistant cells formed when water or nutrients are lacking; the cell copies its chromosome and surrounds it with a multilayer structure. Can stay viable for centuries.
  • Fimbriae: short hairlike projections that stick cells to a substrate or to each other ('Velcro').
  • Pili (sex pili): longer than fimbriae; connect two cells for DNA exchange.
  • Taxis: movement toward or away from a stimulus (chemotaxis = toward/away from chemicals; light can also trigger it). About half of prokaryotes are motile.
  • Prokaryotic flagella have the same function as eukaryotic flagella but differ in structure, propulsion mechanism, and molecular composition. Do not memorize the structure.
  • No complex compartmentalization (no membrane-bound organelles), but infolded specialized membranes carry out respiration (aerobic prokaryote) or photosynthesis (photosynthetic prokaryote).
  • One circular chromosome in the nucleoid (region with no membrane). Eukaryotes have multiple linear chromosomes. Prokaryotes have less DNA and fewer proteins.
  • Plasmids: smaller rings of independently replicating DNA; exchanged through pili.
  • Differences in ribosomes and in DNA replication, transcription, translation let some antibiotics kill bacteria without harming human cells.
  • Binary fission every 1 to 3 hours under optimal conditions; fastest about every 20 minutes.
  • Three key features: small size, binary fission, short generation times.

Terms

prokaryote
Single-celled organism without a nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; domains Bacteria and Archaea.
cocci / bacilli / spirals
Sphere-shaped / rod-shaped / spiral-shaped prokaryotes.
peptidoglycan
Bacterial cell wall material made of sugars cross-linked by polypeptides.
Gram stain
A stain that classifies bacteria as Gram-positive or Gram-negative by cell wall composition.
Gram-positive
Bacteria with a thick peptidoglycan wall; stain purple.
Gram-negative
Bacteria with a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane; stain pink/red; more antibiotic resistant.
capsule
A dense, sticky polysaccharide or protein layer around the cell wall.
endospore
A dormant, resistant cell formed inside a bacterium to survive harsh conditions.
fimbriae
Short hairlike projections for attaching to surfaces or other cells.
pili (sex pili)
Longer appendages that connect two cells for DNA transfer.
taxis
Movement toward or away from a stimulus.
nucleoid
Region of a prokaryotic cell that holds the circular chromosome, with no membrane around it.
plasmid
Small ring of DNA that replicates independently of the chromosome.
binary fission
Asexual division of a prokaryotic cell into two identical cells.

Examples

  • 'Curse of the pyramids': people entering Egyptian pyramids got sick and died from bacterial endospores.
  • Oreo analogy: Gram-negative wall is like an Oreo (outer lipopolysaccharide membrane and inner membrane with thin peptidoglycan between). Gram-positive is an Oreo with one cookie removed (thick peptidoglycan, no outer membrane).

Big idea
Three factors produce the high genetic diversity of prokaryotes: rapid reproduction, mutation, and genetic recombination. Diverse nutritional and metabolic adaptations have evolved in prokaryotes

Lecture video 27.3 (opens online)
Big idea
Prokaryotes are classified by energy source (phototrophs use light, chemotrophs use chemicals) and carbon source (autotrophs use CO2, heterotrophs need organic nutrients), giving four nutritional modes. Some fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, as in the heterocysts of Anabaena, and many cooperate in biofilms.

Must know

  • Energy source: phototrophs (light) vs chemotrophs (chemicals).

27.4

Prokaryotes have radiated into a diverse set of lineages

Lecture video 27.4 (opens online)
Big idea
Prokaryotes date to about 3.5 billion years ago and inhabit every environment that supports life. Genetic analysis in the 1970s split them into Bacteria and Archaea. Bacteria include Proteobacteria, Chlamydias, Spirochetes, Cyanobacteria (all Gram-negative) and Gram-positive bacteria. Archaea share traits with both bacteria and eukaryotes and include extremophiles (extreme halophiles, extreme thermophiles) and methanogens, organized into Euryarchaeota, the TACK supergroup, and Lokiarchaeotes.

Must know

  • Prokaryotes date back about 3.5 billion years; first organisms on Earth.
  • Genetic analysis in the 1970s divided prokaryotes into Bacteria and Archaea.
  • About 16,000 prokaryotic species named; 700,000 to 1.4 million bacterial species estimated. A handful of soil may hold about 10,000 species. Metagenomics samples all genomes in an environment.
  • Every major mode of nutrition and metabolism is found among bacteria.
  • Proteobacteria: Gram-negative; include photoautotrophs, chemoautotrophs, heterotrophs. Pathogens: Neisseria gonorrhoeae (gonorrhea), Vibrio cholerae (cholera), Helicobacter pylori (stomach ulcers).
  • Chlamydias: all parasitize animal cells; Gram-negative but walls lack peptidoglycan. Chlamydia trachomatis causes nongonococcal urethritis, one of the most common STDs in the US.
  • Spirochetes: helical, Gram-negative heterotrophs; move by rotating internal filaments; many free-living, some pathogens. Treponema (syphilis), Borrelia (Lyme disease, carried by ticks). Know genus only.
  • Cyanobacteria: Gram-negative photoautotrophs; solitary or filamentous; freshwater and marine.
  • Gram-positive bacteria: very diverse. Actinomycetes form colonies; include pathogens and soil decomposers. Streptomyces are cultured as a source of antibiotics. Pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus anthracis (anthrax), Clostridium botulinum (botulism, paralysis, source of Botox).
  • Only the Gram-positive group is Gram-positive; the other four bacterial groups covered are Gram-negative.
  • Archaea share some traits with bacteria, some with eukaryotes, and have unique traits.
  • Three-domain comparison: nuclear envelope and membrane-enclosed organelles only in Eukarya; peptidoglycan only in Bacteria; branched hydrocarbons in membrane lipids only in Archaea; one kind of RNA polymerase in Bacteria vs several kinds in Archaea and Eukarya; initiator amino acid formyl-methionine in Bacteria vs methionine in Archaea and Eukarya.
  • Extreme halophiles: tolerate or require very salty environments.
  • Extreme thermophiles: tolerate very high temperatures (even over 100 degrees C), such as hot springs.
  • Methanogens: obligate anaerobes that produce methane as a by-product; found under ice in Greenland, in swamps and marshes, and in guts of cattle, termites, and other herbivores.
  • Euryarchaeota: extreme halophiles, most methanogens, some extreme thermophiles.
  • TACK supergroup: Thaumarchaeota, Aigarchaeota, Crenarchaeota, Korarchaeota. Crenarchaeota includes the rest of the extreme thermophiles.
  • Lokiarchaeotes: closely related to TACK archaea; may be the sister group of eukaryotes.

28.2

Excavates include protists with modified mitochondria and protists with unique flagella

Lecture video 28.2 (opens online)
Big idea
Excavata is defined by its cytoskeleton; some members have an excavated feeding groove on one side of the body. It has three monophyletic groups (clades): diplomonads, parabasalids and euglenozoans. Diplomonads and parabasalids lack plastids, have reduced mitochondria and mostly live in anaerobic environments. Euglenozoans have a spiral or crystalline rod inside each flagellum and split into kinetoplastids and euglenids.

Must know

  • Excavata: characterized by its CYTOSKELETON; some have an excavated FEEDING GROOVE (a trench along one side of the body); not all members have it.
  • Three clades "I do want you to know": DIPLOMONADS, PARABASALIDS, EUGLENOZOANS.
  • Diplomonads and parabasalids: no plastids, reduced mitochondria, mostly anaerobic environments (no oxygen).
  • Diplomonads: reduced mitochondria called MITOSOMES that lack electron transport chains, so energy comes from anaerobic pathways. Two equal-sized nuclei and multiple flagella. Many are parasites: Giardia intestinalis (intestinal infections in mammals).
  • Parabasalids: reduced mitochondria called HYDROGENOSOMES that make some energy anaerobically and release HYDROGEN GAS as a by-product.
  • Trichomonas vaginalis: parabasalid, sexually transmitted parasite with an UNDULATING MEMBRANE; travels the reproductive and urinary tracts and feeds on the vaginal lining; about 140 million people per year (lecture figure).
  • Euglenozoans: very diverse (predatory heterotrophs, photosynthetic autotrophs, mixotrophs, parasites). Distinguishing feature: a SPIRAL or CRYSTALLINE ROD inside each flagellum.
  • Euglenozoa is a MAJOR GROUP with two clades: KINETOPLASTIDS and EUGLENIDS.
  • Kinetoplastids: a single mitochondrion with an organized mass of DNA called a KINETOPLAST. Free-living ones eat prokaryotes in freshwater, marine and moist land habitats; some are parasites.
  • Trypanosoma (kinetoplastid, seen in lab): causes SLEEPING SICKNESS; Chagas disease (also Trypanosoma) can lead to congestive heart failure and is on the rise. Sleeping sickness infects about 10,000 people a year.
  • Euglenids: 1 or 2 flagella emerge from a POCKET at one end of the cell; some are mixotrophs that switch between autotrophic and heterotrophic modes. Euglena: flexible PELLICLE and a red EYESPOT (light detector).

28.3

The "SAR" clade is a highly diverse group of protists defined by DNA similarities

Lecture video 28.3 (opens online)
Big idea
SAR is grouped by DNA similarity and has three major groups. Stramenopiles (hairy + smooth flagellum) include diatoms, brown algae and oomycetes. Alveolates (alveoli, sacs under the plasma membrane) include dinoflagellates, apicomplexans and ciliates. Rhizarians are mostly amoebas with thread-like pseudopodia: radiolarians, forams and cercozoans. The Plasmodium (malaria) life cycle is the one life cycle to know.

Must know

  • SAR: grouped by DNA similarities. Three MAJOR GROUPS: Stramenopiles, Alveolates, Rhizarians.
  • STRAMENOPILES: some of the most important photosynthetic organisms on Earth; most have a HAIRY flagellum paired with a SMOOTH flagellum. Clades: diatoms, oomycetes, brown algae.
  • DIATOMS: unicellular algae with a unique two-part wall of SILICON DIOXIDE (glass-like). Walls resist high pressure, protecting against predators. Diatomaceous earth (pool filters). About 100,000 species; marine and freshwater PHYTOPLANKTON; so abundant their photosynthesis affects global CO2.
  • BROWN ALGAE: largest and most complex multicellular algae; ALL multicellular; brown color from CAROTENOIDS; mostly marine; the seaweeds.
  • Brown alga body: HOLDFAST (root-like, anchors to rock), STIPE (stem-like), BLADES (leaf-like), gas-filled FLOATS (keep blades near the surface). Plant-like but NO true tissues or organs.
  • Brown algae as food: Laminaria is eaten; ALGINATE (gel in the cell wall) thickens processed foods.
  • OOMYCETES: water molds, white rusts, downy mildews. Once classified as fungi (multinucleate filaments look like hyphae), but cell walls are CELLULOSE, not chitin, and DNA shows they are not closely related to fungi. No plastids, no photosynthesis; parasites or decomposers.
  • Phytophthora infestans: oomycete causing POTATO LATE BLIGHT.
  • ALVEOLATES: named for ALVEOLI, membrane-enclosed sacs just under the plasma membrane. Clades: dinoflagellates, apicomplexans, ciliates.
  • DINOFLAGELLATES: marine and freshwater phytoplankton; TWO FLAGELLA in grooves of CELLULOSE PLATES; the beating makes them SPIN.
  • RED TIDE = dinoflagellate bloom; water looks red, pink or brownish from carotenoids; toxic, kills invertebrates and fish and can poison people who eat contaminated seafood; becoming more common, ocean warming is thought to be a factor.
  • APICOMPLEXANS: nearly all are animal PARASITES; spread as SPOROZOITES; the APEX holds a complex of organelles for penetrating host cells; life cycles have sexual and asexual stages and need TWO OR MORE HOSTS.
  • Plasmodium (apicomplexan) causes MALARIA; hosts are mosquitoes and humans; about 220 million infected and up to 450,000 deaths per year; the first licensed malaria vaccine began routine use in Africa in 2019.
  • PLASMODIUM LIFE CYCLE (know it; extra-credit question): mosquito bite injects SPOROZOITES (n) → human LIVER cells → MEROZOITES (n) released → infect RED BLOOD CELLS → GAMETOCYTES (n, male and female) → taken up by another mosquito → GAMETES → fertilization → ZYGOTE (2n, the only diploid stage) → MEIOSIS → OOCYST → sporozoites. Sexual stages in the MOSQUITO; asexual stages in the HUMAN.
  • CILIATES: use CILIA to move and feed; cilia cover the whole surface (Paramecium, oral groove) or are clustered in rows or tufts (Stentor).
  • Ciliates have two kinds of nuclei: tiny MICRONUCLEI and large MACRONUCLEI. CONJUGATION exchanges micronuclei = genetic variation WITHOUT reproduction. Asexual reproduction = BINARY FISSION (know this; skip the nuclear details).
  • RHIZARIANS: many are amoebas with THREAD-LIKE pseudopodia. Clades: radiolarians, forams, cercozoans.
  • RADIOLARIANS: symmetrical internal skeletons usually of SILICA (glass); mostly marine.
  • FORAMS (foraminiferans): porous CALCIUM CARBONATE shells called TESTS; pseudopodia through the pores for swimming, test formation and feeding; some host photosynthetic algae; marine and freshwater; their fossils form much of the marine sediment.
  • CERCOZOANS: amoeboid and flagellated, thread-like pseudopodia; marine, freshwater and soil; mostly heterotrophic parasites or predators.

28.4

Red algae and green algae are the closest relatives of plants

Lecture video 28.4 (opens online)
Big idea
Archaeplastida contains red algae (a clade), green algae (a major group of chlorophytes and charophytes) and plants. Plastids arose when a heterotrophic protist took in a cyanobacterial endosymbiont; its photosynthetic descendants became the red and green algae, and plants descended from green algae. Red algae get their color from phycoerythrin; green algae have plant-like green chloroplasts.

Must know

  • Archaeplastida = red algae (clade) + green algae (major group: CHLOROPHYTES and CHAROPHYTES) + plants.
  • Red and green algae are the closest relatives of plants; plants descended from green algae.
  • Plastids arose by ENDOSYMBIOSIS: a heterotrophic protist acquired a CYANOBACTERIUM; its descendants evolved into the red and green algal lineages.
  • RED ALGAE: accessory pigment PHYCOERYTHRIN masks chlorophyll's green; color varies with depth (greenish red in shallow water, dark red to almost black in deep water).
  • Most red algae are multicellular; the largest are seaweeds. Reproduction is SEXUAL; life cycles show ALTERNATION OF GENERATIONS.
  • Red algae live in WARM coastal waters of tropical oceans; some are eaten: Porphyra (nori) wraps sushi.
  • GREEN ALGAE: green chloroplasts structurally and chemically like plants'.
  • Green algae are a PARAPHYLETIC group (charophytes + chlorophytes); not monophyletic.
  • CHAROPHYTES are the algae most closely related to plants.
  • CHLOROPHYTES: mostly freshwater, also marine and some terrestrial; some unicellular and free-living, some symbiotic; often in high visible and UV light.
  • Three ways green algae got larger and more complex: (1) COLONIES of individual cells (Pediastrum), (2) true MULTICELLULAR bodies by cell division and differentiation (Volvox and Ulva, sea lettuce), (3) repeated nuclear division with NO cytoplasmic division, one huge multinucleate cell (Caulerpa, invasive).
  • Most chlorophytes have complex life cycles with both sexual and asexual stages.

Unikonts include protists that are closely related to fungi and animals

Lecture video 28.5 (opens online)
Big idea
Unikonta contains animals, fungi and related protists in two major groups: Amoebozoans (amoebas with lobe- or tube-shaped pseudopodia: tubulinids, slime molds, entamoebas) and Opisthokonts (animals, fungi, choanoflagellates, nucleariids). One controversial hypothesis puts unikonts as the first eukaryotes to diverge.

Must know

  • Unikonta: two MAJOR GROUPS: AMOEBOZOANS and OPISTHOKONTS.
  • Amoebozoans: amoebas with LOBE- or TUBE-shaped pseudopodia (rhizarians have THREAD-like ones). Clades: TUBULINIDS, SLIME MOLDS, ENTAMOEBAS.
  • Opisthokonts: ANIMALS, FUNGI and related protists (choanoflagellates, nucleariids).
  • Root of the eukaryotic tree is uncertain; one controversial hypothesis: unikonts diverged first, so they may be most like the common ancestor of eukaryotes.
  • TUBULINIDS: diverse amoebozoans (e.g. Amoeba proteus); soil, freshwater and marine; predators or detritus feeders (detritus = dead material on the bottom).
  • SLIME MOLDS (mycetozoans): once thought to be fungi because they make spores; the resemblance is CONVERGENT EVOLUTION, not homology.
  • PLASMODIAL slime molds: bright yellow or orange; feeding mass = PLASMODIUM, ONE huge cell with many DIPLOID nuclei and no membranes dividing it; when conditions worsen it forms FRUITING BODIES for sexual reproduction.
  • CELLULAR slime molds: feeding stage = solitary cells; when conditions worsen they aggregate into a slug-like mass (cells stay separated by membranes) → fruiting body → spores → amoebas. Not in lab.
  • ENTAMOEBAS: parasites of vertebrates and some invertebrates; humans host at least 6 species but only Entamoeba histolytica is pathogenic: AMOEBIC DYSENTERY, the 3rd leading cause of death from eukaryotic parasites.

28.6

Protists play key roles in ecological communities

Lecture video 28.6 (opens online)
Big idea
Protists live in aquatic and moist terrestrial habitats and act as symbionts (mutualists or parasites) or as producers. Photosynthetic protists and prokaryotes are the main producers in aquatic communities. Warming sea surfaces reduce the growth of photosynthetic protists, which can affect fisheries and the carbon cycle.

Must know

  • Two key ecological roles: SYMBIONTS and PRODUCERS.
  • Mutualists: dinoflagellates inside coral polyps nourish reef-building corals (the coral gives them a home); protists in TERMITE guts digest wood (termites can't alone).
  • Parasites: Plasmodium (malaria); Pfiesteria, a dinoflagellate that attacks fish skin; Phytophthora (sudden oak death).
  • Producers: use light (or inorganic compounds) to turn CO2 into organic compounds; photosynthetic protists + prokaryotes are the MAIN PRODUCERS in aquatic communities.
  • All other organisms are consumers that feed on producers directly (herbivorous plankton) or indirectly (carnivorous plankton, animals).
  • Warmer sea surface → less growth of photosynthetic protists and prokaryotes, because warm surface water keeps cold, nutrient-rich water from rising. Effects: fishery yields and the global carbon cycle.

Traps (ch26-27)
  • Homology (shared ancestry) vs analogy (convergent evolution). Similar looks do not mean close relationship: glass lizards look like snakes but are not their close relatives; Australian and African golden moles look alike by convergence.
  • Paraphyletic INCLUDES the common ancestor but leaves out some descendants. Polyphyletic LEAVES OUT the most recent common ancestor. Only monophyletic groups are clades.
  • Order of tip labels means nothing: rotating branches does not change relationships. Read branch points, not neighbors. A taxon did not evolve from the taxon next to it.
  • Trees show pattern of descent, not phenotypic similarity, and (unless branch lengths are proportional) not when species evolved or how much change happened.
  • Basal taxon diverged early; it is not 'primitive' or the ancestor of the others.
  • Shared ancestral vs shared derived is relative: a backbone is ancestral within vertebrates but derived relative to other animals.
  • Outgroup is closely related but not part of the ingroup. Characters shared by outgroup and ingroup are ancestral.

Define monophyletic, paraphyletic, and polyphyletic groups. Draw one tree and circle an example of each. Give a real example of a paraphyletic and a polyphyletic group from lecture.

  • Monophyletic: ancestor and all descendants; the only kind of group that is a clade.
  • Paraphyletic: ancestor and some, but not all, descendants.
  • Polyphyletic: distantly related species without their most recent common ancestor.
  • Paraphyletic example: even-toed ungulates plus hippos without cetaceans.
  • Polyphyletic example: cetaceans plus seals grouped by appearance.

Distinguish homology from analogy. Explain why systematists must separate them before building a tree, how they tell them apart, and give examples of each from lecture and the videos.

  • Homology: similarity due to shared ancestry (phenotypic or genetic).
  • Analogy: similarity due to convergent evolution in unrelated species facing similar selection pressures.
  • Only features resulting from common ancestry are useful for inferring phylogeny.
  • More complex similarity makes homology more likely; statistical tools separate molecular homology from coincidental matches.
  • Homology examples: human, bat, whale forelimbs; insect mouthparts and antennae.
  • Analogy examples: Australian mole and African golden mole; bird and insect wings; Astrophytum and Euphorbia; anole body types on different islands.
  • Morphology and genes can disagree (Hawaiian silverswords).

Compare the cell walls of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Explain how the Gram stain distinguishes them, why Gram-negative bacteria are more resistant to antibiotics, why peptidoglycan-targeting antibiotics do not harm humans, and how the Gram-negative wall relates to endotoxins.

  • Bacterial walls contain peptidoglycan (sugars cross-linked by polypeptides); archaeal walls lack it.
  • Gram-positive: thick peptidoglycan, no outer membrane, stains purple.
  • Gram-negative: thin peptidoglycan plus outer lipopolysaccharide membrane, stains pink/red.
  • Outer membrane blocks antibiotics from reaching peptidoglycan, so Gram-negative cells are more resistant.
  • Human cells lack peptidoglycan, so these antibiotics target bacteria only.
  • Endotoxins are the lipopolysaccharide of the Gram-negative outer membrane, released when the cells die (Salmonella).
  • Cell wall functions: maintain shape, protect, prevent bursting in hypotonic environments.

Prokaryotes reproduce asexually by binary fission, yet their populations are highly diverse. Explain the three factors that produce this diversity and describe the three mechanisms of genetic recombination.

  • Binary fission yields identical cells; generation times of 1 to 3 hours (as fast as 20 minutes).
  • Rapid reproduction: huge populations quickly.
  • Mutation: low rates but rapid accumulation due to short generations and large populations.
  • Genetic recombination combines DNA from two sources; between species it is horizontal gene transfer.
  • Transformation: uptake of foreign DNA from surroundings (nonpathogenic cell becomes pathogenic).
  • Transduction: bacteriophages carry genes between hosts.
  • Conjugation: one-way DNA transfer from donor to recipient through a pilus; plasmids can transfer.

Describe the four nutritional modes of prokaryotes and give an example of each type of organism from lecture. Then explain oxygen relations and nitrogen fixation, including the role of heterocysts.

  • Photo vs chemo = energy source (light vs chemicals); auto vs hetero = carbon source (CO2 vs organic).
  • Photoautotroph: light + CO2 (cyanobacteria).
  • Chemoautotroph: inorganic chemicals + CO2 (sulfur bacteria, hydrothermal vent bacteria).
  • Photoheterotroph: light + organic carbon.
  • Chemoheterotroph: organic compounds for both (decomposers, pathogens).
  • Obligate aerobes need oxygen; obligate anaerobes are poisoned by it; facultative anaerobes use it if present or ferment.
  • Nitrogen fixation: N2 to NH3; needed for amino acids and nucleic acids; Anabaena heterocysts fix nitrogen for the filament.

Describe the life cycle of Plasmodium. Name each stage, its host and ploidy, and say which part is sexual and which is asexual.

  • Infected mosquito bites a human and injects sporozoites (n).
  • Sporozoites enter liver cells; liver cells release merozoites (n).
  • Merozoites infect red blood cells and multiply (asexual); some become male and female gametocytes (n).
  • Another mosquito takes up gametocytes with a blood meal; they become gametes in the mosquito.
  • Fertilization makes the zygote (2n), the only diploid stage; meiosis → oocyst → sporozoites that move to the salivary glands.
  • Sexual stages in the mosquito; asexual stages in the human; two-host (complex) life cycle; apicomplexan.

Name the four supergroups of eukaryotes and give the major groups or clades and one example in each.
